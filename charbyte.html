Capability Is Organization

The AI industry is spending a trillion dollars to make models bigger. The larger gains have come from organizing computation better, and that axis is barely touched. I proved it in security, the one place results cannot be faked: 67 CVEs, ranked second in the world. This is where it leads.

There are two ways to make an AI model more capable. One is to make it bigger. The other is to change how the computation it already contains is organized and run, with the weights left fixed. The industry has spent roughly a trillion dollars on the first and almost nothing on the second. The second is where I work, and it already produces results that cannot be argued with.

I proved it in security, because security cannot be graded by the person making the claim. A vulnerability reproduces on a real machine or it does not. Driving commodity models with this method, not frontier models, I found 67 publicly credited vulnerabilities in Apple, Microsoft, and Google software, across kernel memory corruption, browser information disclosure, and privilege escalation, and ranked second in the world in Apple's 2026 product-security research. Every finding was confirmed by execution.

The method acquires a compact, reusable description of how a model's components behave, then runs those components in new arrangements that satisfy a new requirement. No new parameters, no retraining. The weights stay fixed. The model does more because its computation is organized better, not because there is more of it.

The same capability reaches past finding flaws. One form, which I call neural sandboxing, runs an unknown file through a neural network that emulates its behavior and reads the network's internal activity to recover what the file does, without ever running it. Another form adds capability to a frozen model: in a public benchmark, it improved a frozen 0.6-billion-parameter model by 33 and 49 percent on two held-out tasks, weights unchanged, every existing program still working, reproducible from released code.

The leverage is in organization

The largest gains in AI have already come from organization, not size. Between 2014 and 2023, better algorithms improved language-model performance by the equivalent of 22,000 times more compute. Before that, the compute needed to reach a fixed image-recognition result fell 44 times in seven years from algorithms alone, faster than the hardware. Compute is bought in a straight line: twice the chips, roughly twice the cost. Organization compounds. The industry put its money on the straight line and left the compounding curve open.

Shaping is the far end of that curve: more from the same computation, weights frozen, no training at all. It takes what a model already learned and gets more out of it.

Security becomes the defender's game

For as long as software has existed, the attacker has held the advantage. One flaw defeats a million lines of defense; the defender has to be right everywhere, the attacker right once. This method turns that over. It finds and proves real flaws across a whole system, cheaply and continuously, so software can be audited the way a compiler checks a program: in full, on every change, each finding proven rather than guessed. Public crash reports get reproduced automatically and confirmed before anyone can weaponize them. The flood of machine-generated fake reports gets filtered by the only test that matters, whether the bug actually fires.

I did this alone, with ordinary models, against the most scrutinized software in the world. The same engine standing inside every vendor and every codebase changes who is ahead. Better tools reach attackers too, but proof favors whoever owns the code and can fix it, and that is the defender.

Capability comes off the data center

The dominant path to capability compounds in cost: each increment wants a larger model, more compute, more energy, more capital, and the bill returns at the next model. Much of today's investment, and its power demand, is this one curve.

Organization breaks the cycle. Today a model is trained, used, and discarded when a newer one arrives, and the next capability costs another training run. When capability is grown by reorganizing weights that already exist, an existing model stops being something you replace and becomes raw material. Even an outdated model is a substrate you build on, not something you throw away.

Capability then runs on commodity hardware, and on models small enough to fit on a device in your hand, for work that used to require the frontier. The benchmark shows the shape of it: more capability from the same weights, at no additional training cost. The result is lower lifetime energy and cost per unit of useful capability, and less pressure to keep expanding models and data centers as demand grows. This does not replace large models. It puts a second, far cheaper lever beside them.

AI that can be trusted where it cannot be today

AI is kept out of medicine, law, infrastructure, and auditing not because it lacks knowledge, but because it cannot be trusted to say "there is nothing wrong here" and be right. A model that fabricates a clean result is worse than none, so wherever a false "all clear" is catastrophic, AI stays at arm's length.

Security is the hardest version of that problem, and the one I have proof on. A finding counts only when execution confirms it, by a test that cannot be argued into a yes. The discipline that makes a vulnerability undeniable is the discipline that makes a clean result trustworthy: not a model's opinion that something is safe, but a ground-truth test that it is. That opens the domains that exclude AI today.

Capability that spreads rather than concentrates

The advantage lives in the acquired description, not in the weights, so it is portable and model-agnostic. It does not expire when the next model ships; a better model becomes a better substrate for the same description. The descriptions accumulate and carry forward, improving as open models improve.

That makes capability behave like a public library that compounds, not a private asset that depreciates. Advanced ability stops being locked behind the few organizations that can afford the largest models and reaches anyone with a commodity model and the right structure over it.

Dual-use, and how I handle it

The same engine that finds a flaw in order to fix it can find a flaw in order to exploit it. A capability this general is dual-use, and I treat it that way. My security work is coordinated disclosure on the defender's side: findings go to the vendor, proven and then fixed, never sold and never dropped in public. That line is drawn, and I hold it in the open.

Where this goes

None of this came from theory. I built the method to do work that could not be faked, and it held, in security first and everywhere I have pointed it since. Scale is one axis of AI, and it is crowded and expensive. Organization is the other, and it is wide open. That is the one I am building on. ■
