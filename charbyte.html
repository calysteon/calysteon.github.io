Capability Is Organization

Most work on AI capability adds scale. I found that you can add capability by organizing a model's existing computation instead, with the weights left fixed, and I proved it in security, where results cannot be faked. This is a concrete attempt to describe what that could become.

I spend my time on problems that either work or they do not. A vulnerability reproduces on a real machine or it does not. There is no partial credit in security, and no room for a flattering story. That is not the temperament of someone prone to hype, which is exactly why I want to be careful here: I think the upside of one specific idea is larger than it looks, and I would rather describe it concretely and risk being wrong than keep it vague and safe.

The idea is this. There are two ways to make an AI model more capable. One is to make it bigger. The other is to change how its existing computation is organized and run, with the weights left fixed. The industry has spent roughly a trillion dollars developing the first and almost nothing developing the second. I have spent the last few years on the second, and I proved it works in the one place where results cannot be faked.

What follows is an attempt to describe what that could become. I will start with what is already true, because the rest only matters if the floor is solid.

What is already true

The method acquires a small, reusable description of how a model's components behave, and uses that description to run the components in new arrangements that meet a new requirement. No new parameters. No retraining. The weights stay fixed. The model does more because its computation is organized better, not because there is more of it.

I tested this in security because security cannot be self-graded. Driving commodity models with the method, not frontier models, produced 67 publicly credited CVEs across Apple, Microsoft, and Google, spanning kernel memory corruption, browser information disclosure, and privilege escalation, and ranked me second in the world in Apple's 2026 product-security research. Every one of those findings was confirmed by execution, not asserted.

The same capability extends past finding flaws. One form of it, which I call neural sandboxing, runs an unknown file through a neural network that emulates its behavior and reads the network's internal activity to recover what the file does, without ever running it. Another form adds capability to a frozen model: in a public benchmark, it improved a frozen 0.6-billion-parameter model by 33 and 49 percent on two held-out tasks, with the weights unchanged and every existing program still working, reproducible from released code.

That is the floor. None of it is a forecast. Everything after this is.

Two directions, not one

We have been measuring AI progress along a single axis: size. Bigger models, more compute, more data, larger data centers. It has worked, and it is why the investment is what it is. But size is one direction, and there is a second that is barely explored: how well a fixed amount of computation is organized.

This is not a hunch. Some of the largest gains in AI have already come from organization, not size. Between 2014 and 2023, better algorithms alone improved language-model performance by the equivalent of an estimated 22,000 times more compute. Before that, the compute needed to reach a fixed image-recognition result fell 44 times in seven years from better algorithms alone, faster than the hardware improved over the same span. Compute is bought in a straight line: twice the chips, roughly twice the cost. Organization compounds. The field has poured its money into the straight line and left the compounding curve comparatively untouched.

Those gains came from better ways to train. Shaping takes the same principle past training entirely: more from the same computation, with the weights frozen and no new training run at all. It takes what a model already learned and gets more out of it. Everything below is a guess about what happens when that direction is developed the way scale has been. A concrete guess is more useful than a careful silence, so I will be specific, and specific things can turn out wrong.

Security becomes the defender's game

For as long as software has existed, the attacker has held the advantage. One flaw can defeat a million lines of defense; the defender has to be right everywhere while the attacker has to be right once. Security has mostly been the work of catching up, and lately it has gotten harder, because vendors are now buried under machine-generated vulnerability reports that read plausibly and reproduce nothing.

A method that can find and prove real flaws across a whole system, cheaply and continuously, starts to turn that advantage over. A system could be audited the way a compiler checks a program: in full, on every change, each finding proven rather than guessed. Public crash reports could be reproduced automatically and confirmed as real before anyone turns them into a weapon. The flood of fake reports could be filtered by the only test that matters, which is whether the bug actually fires.

I am one person, driving ordinary models, and those 67 findings are in the most scrutinized software in the world. The honest version of the upside is just that, continuous and everywhere: the same engine standing inside every vendor and every codebase that matters. The caveat is that better tools reach attackers too. But proof favors whoever owns the code and can fix it, and that is the defender.

Capability comes off the data center

The dominant path to capability is expensive in a way that compounds. Each new increment of ability wants a larger model, which wants more compute, more energy, and more capital, and then the same bill arrives at the next model. A large share of today's investment, and its power demand, is this one curve.

There is also a quieter cost. Today a model is trained, used, and then discarded when a newer one arrives, and the price of the next capability is another training run from scratch. Adding capability by organization changes that. If new ability can be grown by reorganizing weights that already exist, an existing model stops being something you replace and becomes raw material. Even an outdated model is a substrate you build the next capability on top of, rather than throw away.

So capability can come from commodity hardware, and from models small enough to run on a device in your hand, for work that used to require the frontier. The benchmark is an early result, but it is the shape of the thing: more capability from the same weights, at no additional training cost. The aim, in the end, is lower lifetime energy and cost for each unit of useful capability, which would ease the pressure to keep expanding models and data centers as demand grows. This does not replace large models. It adds a second, far cheaper lever beside them.

AI that can be trusted where it cannot be today

The reason AI is kept out of medicine, law, infrastructure, and auditing is not that it lacks knowledge. It is that it cannot be trusted to say "there is nothing wrong here" and be right. A model that fabricates a clean bill of health is worse than no model at all, so wherever a false "all clear" is catastrophic, AI is held at arm's length.

Security is the hardest version of that problem, and the one I have the most evidence on. A finding only counts when it is confirmed by execution, by a check that cannot be argued into a yes. The same discipline that makes a vulnerability undeniable is what could make a clean result trustworthy: not a model's opinion that something is safe, but a ground-truth test that it is. If that generalizes, AI becomes usable in exactly the domains that exclude it now. I want to be clear that this is hard and specific to each domain, and that security is a proof it is possible, not a proof it is easy.

Capability that spreads rather than concentrates

There is one more consequence, and it is the most speculative, so treat it as the furthest reach. Because the advantage lives in the acquired description rather than in the weights, it is portable and model-agnostic. It does not expire when the next model ships; a better model simply becomes a better substrate for the same description. And the descriptions accumulate. They carry forward and improve as open models improve.

That makes capability behave less like a private asset that depreciates and more like a public library that compounds. Advanced ability would not stay locked behind the few organizations that can afford the largest models. It would reach anyone with a commodity model and the right reusable structure over it. I have taken this a short distance so far, and it is the part most likely to be wrong, but it is also the part I would most want to be right.

What this asks of whoever builds it

I should say plainly that the same engine which finds a flaw in order to fix it can find a flaw in order to exploit it. A capability general enough to do everything above is dual-use by nature, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

So the discipline has to be built in from the start, not added later. My security work is coordinated disclosure on the defender's side: findings go to the vendor, proven and then fixed, never sold and never dropped in public. I would rather hold that line openly, and accept the limits it puts on the work, than tell a cleaner story.

What it adds up to

I did not reason my way to any of this. I built a thing to do real work under conditions I could not fake, watched it hold, pointed it at other problems, and watched it hold there too. Everything past the security results is a forecast, and the parts furthest from security are the most likely to be wrong.

If even part of it holds, AI gets cheaper to run, usable in places it is shut out of now, and less tied to whoever owns the biggest cluster. I am not sure which of the forecasts are right. I am sure about the floor, because I built it and it holds, and that is why I think the rest is worth chasing. ■
